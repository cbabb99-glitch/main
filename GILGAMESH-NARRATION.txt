THE EPIC OF GILGAMESH
Narration Script — Standard Babylonian Version
19 Scenes

---

SCENE 1 — The Creation of Enkidu
The gods looked down on Uruk and saw a king running wild. Gilgamesh was two-thirds divine, stronger than any mortal who ever lived — and he was crushing his own people under the weight of it. So the goddess Aruru knelt in the wilderness, took a handful of clay, and shaped it into a man. She called him Enkidu. He was born covered in hair, running with gazelles, drinking at their side, a creature of the wild with no memory of cities, no language, no name for what he was. The gods had built him for one purpose: to match Gilgamesh — and to stop him.

SCENE 2 — The Tyrant King
In Uruk, behind walls so immense that later ages would swear gods must have built them, Gilgamesh ruled like a storm that never passed. He was the most powerful man alive, and he knew it. No daughter was safe from him, no son spared his temper, no citizen above his will. The people of Uruk prayed — not for rain, not for victory — they prayed for someone strong enough to stand in his way.

SCENE 3 — Taming the Wild Man
The gods sent a woman named Shamhat into the wilderness to find Enkidu. For six days and seven nights she stayed with him, and when it was over, something in him had changed forever. The gazelles he once ran with now fled at his approach — he was no longer one of them. Shamhat told him of Uruk, of its king, of the walls and the feasts and the glory he'd never imagined. Enkidu listened, and for the first time in his life, he wanted something: he wanted to go to the city and challenge the man everyone feared.

SCENE 4 — The Wrestling Match
Enkidu walked into Uruk and blocked Gilgamesh's path in the street. No one in the city had ever dared. The two men came together like mountains colliding — grappling, throwing each other against doorframes, shaking the foundations of the houses around them. It was the fight of the age. And at the end of it, exhausted, neither man had won. Gilgamesh looked at Enkidu — the first person alive who could match him — and instead of killing him, he embraced him. In one afternoon, Uruk's tyrant found his first friend.

SCENE 5 — The Call to Glory
Friendship changed Gilgamesh, but it didn't tame his hunger for greatness. With Enkidu beside him, he decided the two of them would do something no men had done: march into the Cedar Forest, home of the gods, and kill the monster that guarded it. Enkidu had lived near that forest. He knew exactly what they'd be walking into — and he tried, and failed, to talk his friend out of it.

SCENE 6 — Into the Forest
They crossed mountain after mountain, watching the horizon darken as the cedar trees grew closer. Each night Gilgamesh dreamed of falling mountains and dying bulls, and each morning Enkidu told him the dreams meant victory, even when he wasn't sure. Fear didn't stop them. It just walked beside them the whole way.

SCENE 7 — The Battle with Humbaba
Humbaba was the forest's guardian, placed there by the gods themselves — a monster whose roar was floodwater, whose breath was fire, whose face was said to be carved from the coils of its own entrails. When Gilgamesh and Enkidu finally found him, the fight shook the cedar trees to their roots. It took everything both men had. In the end, they beheaded the guardian the gods had trusted to keep mortals out — and the forest that no human had ever touched belonged to them.

SCENE 8 — The Goddess Scorned
Word of the kill made Gilgamesh famous beyond Uruk, and it caught the attention of someone far more dangerous than any monster: the goddess Ishtar. She came to him herself and offered to make him her husband. Gilgamesh refused her to her face, and reminded her — accurately — of every lover she'd destroyed before him. Ishtar went to her father in a fury and demanded the Bull of Heaven to punish him. The bull tore through Uruk's streets, and Gilgamesh and Enkidu brought it down together — but Enkidu had now insulted a god twice in one story. The gods don't forget twice.

SCENE 9 — The Warning Dream
That night, Enkidu dreamed of the gods in council, debating who would pay for Humbaba and the bull. In the dream, it was decided. He woke knowing something Gilgamesh didn't yet: that this story was about to cost one of them everything.

SCENE 10 — The Death of Enkidu
Enkidu fell sick, and nothing stopped it. For days he suffered in a dark room while Gilgamesh stayed beside him, watching his closest friend fade in front of him. When Enkidu finally died, Gilgamesh didn't just grieve — he broke. He had just learned, in the most personal way possible, that strength doesn't save you from loss. If it could happen to Enkidu, it could happen to him too.

SCENE 11 — The Flight from Death
Gilgamesh fled Uruk altogether, terrified of his own mortality for the first time in his life. He'd heard of one man who had escaped death completely — a survivor from a flood so old it predated memory itself — and he decided he would find him, whatever it took. His path led him to a mountain passage guarded by scorpion-men, beings no mortal was meant to approach. He walked into the dark tunnel between them anyway.

SCENE 12 — The Wilderness
Beyond the tunnel lay nothing but desolation — mountains, wasteland, and silence. Gilgamesh walked it alone, half out of his mind with grief, still dressed in the lion skin he'd taken on the road, chasing a rumor that might not even be true.

SCENE 13 — Siduri's Tavern
At the edge of the known world, Gilgamesh found a tavern run by a woman named Siduri, standing at the shore of a sea no living man had crossed. She took one look at him — wild-eyed, grieving, starving — and tried to talk him out of whatever he was chasing. Gilgamesh wouldn't hear it. He told her his whole story and asked the only question that mattered to him anymore: how do I find the man who cheated death?

SCENE 14 — The Waters of Death
Siduri pointed him toward a ferryman named Urshanabi, the only being who knew the way across the Waters of Death — a sea so lethal that touching it meant instant destruction. Gilgamesh cut down an entire forest of punting poles so he'd never need to dip a hand in the water, and pushed off toward the one island left on earth where an immortal still lived.

SCENE 15 — Utnapishtim's Story
On the far shore, Gilgamesh finally met him: Utnapishtim, the only human being who had ever been granted eternal life. Utnapishtim told him the story the gods had tried to erase — of a flood sent to wipe out humanity entirely, and of the single ark that survived it, built on a secret warning from one god who broke the rules to save him. The gods had made Utnapishtim immortal as payment for surviving what should have killed everyone. It was a one-time gift. It had never been repeated. It was not for sale.

SCENE 16 — The Test of the Six Days
Still, Utnapishtim offered him one chance: stay awake for six days and seven nights, and we'll talk about immortality. Gilgamesh agreed instantly — and fell asleep almost as fast. Utnapishtim had his wife bake a loaf of bread for every day he slept, lined up beside him, so that when Gilgamesh woke and tried to claim he'd never closed his eyes, the stale, moldering proof was sitting right there. He couldn't even stay awake for a week. Immortality had never been within reach.

SCENE 17 — The Plant of Youth
Out of pity, Utnapishtim gave him one consolation prize: a thorny plant at the bottom of the sea that could restore youth, if not grant eternal life. Gilgamesh tied stones to his feet, sank to the ocean floor, and tore the plant free with his bare hands. For one moment, he had something to bring home — something real to show for everything he'd lost.

SCENE 18 — The Serpent's Theft
On the journey back, Gilgamesh stopped to bathe in a cool spring and set the plant down on the bank beside him. A serpent slid out of the grass, snatched it, and vanished — gaining the ability to shed its skin and renew itself, while Gilgamesh was left with nothing but his own hands, empty again, on the ground where the plant had been.

SCENE 19 — The Return to Uruk
Gilgamesh walked home with nothing to show for the entire journey — no monster's head, no immortality, no plant, no answers. But as he approached Uruk and looked up, he saw the walls he had built towering over the city he'd once ruled so badly. That would outlast him. The friendship, the forest, the grief, the failure at the edge of the world — all of it had changed him into a king worth being. He couldn't live forever. But the walls would stand, and the story would be told, for four thousand years and counting.
