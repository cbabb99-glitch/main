Lesson 1

How a fire alarm system talks

Every system has three parts. Initiating devices sense a problem (smoke detectors, heat detectors, pull stations, sprinkler waterflow and valve switches). They report to the fire alarm control panel (FACP; NFPA 72 calls it the control unit). The panel decides what the signal means and runs the outputs: horns and strobes, elevator recall, door holders, fan shutdown, and the communicator that sends the signal to a central monitoring station.

Addressable devices sit on a signaling line circuit (SLC) and each one reports its own address, like L1D014. Older conventional systems group devices on an initiating device circuit (IDC) with an end-of-line resistor so the panel can tell if a wire is cut. Horns and strobes run on notification appliance circuits (NACs).

Lesson 2

Three signals, three different jobs

The panel sorts everything into three types. Learn these cold. They decide how urgent the call is and who gets dispatched.

Alarm

There may be a fire. Life safety first.

Comes from
Smoke and heat detectors, pull stations, sprinkler waterflow switches
System does
Sounds horns, flashes strobes, recalls elevators, releases door holders, shuts down fans, and sends the signal for fire department dispatch
You do
Treat every alarm you didn't cause as real. Find the device on the display before you touch a key.

Supervisory

Something that protects the building is off-normal.

Comes from
Sprinkler valve tamper switches, low air on a dry-pipe system, fire pump running, and most duct smoke detectors
System does
Lights the supervisory LED, sounds the panel buzzer, and sends to central, which usually calls the building contact rather than the fire department
You do
Find out why. A closed sprinkler valve means the building may have no sprinkler protection.

Trouble

The system itself has a fault and might not work.

Comes from
Open or cut wires, ground faults, AC power loss, low or missing batteries, a failed communicator, a device missing from its base
System does
Lights the trouble LED, sounds the buzzer, and reports trouble to central
You do
Document it, then troubleshoot it. Trouble usually clears itself once the fault is repaired.
Acknowledge, Silence and Reset do different things. Acknowledge tells the panel a person has seen the event and quiets the panel's own buzzer. Silence turns off the building's horns, but the panel is still in alarm. Reset clears latched alarms, and it only works once the device has been restored: the smoke has cleared, or the pull station has been reset with its key. If a new alarm comes in after you silence, the horns sound again.
Lesson 3

Putting a system on test

The most common rookie mistake in this trade costs nothing to avoid: testing a monitored system without calling it in. The central station sees a real alarm, dispatches the fire department, and the building may get billed for a false alarm. Follow the same order every time.

Before

  1. Check in with the building contact. Ask who is in the building and about any known problems.
  2. Look at the panel before touching it. Photograph and write down any existing alarm, supervisory or trouble conditions.
  3. Find out what the system controls: elevator recall, door holders, fans and dampers, access control, suppression releasing. Coordinate or bypass these per company procedure and with the owner's OK.
  4. Call central station and place the account on test. Notify occupants, and the fire department if your area requires it.

During

  1. Test each device and confirm the panel shows the right signal type and location text.
  2. Confirm central station received each signal type. If they didn't, the building isn't really monitored.
  3. Write down every deficiency as you find it.
  4. An alarm you didn't cause is a real alarm until proven otherwise.

After

  1. Restore every device: reset pull stations, clear detectors, open valves back up.
  2. Un-bypass every output you disabled.
  3. Reset the panel and confirm it is normal, apart from conditions you already documented.
  4. Call central: compare the signals they logged with what you tested, then take the account off test.
  5. Review the results with the building contact and leave the report.
What the call sounds like
"Hi, this is Jordan with [Company], tech ID 4471. I'm at Harbor Point Medical Offices, account 20-11873, passcode is [passcode]. I need the whole account on test for three hours for an annual inspection. We'll be sending alarm, supervisory and trouble signals. Can I get your operator number?" — Operator: "You're on test until 11:15. Operator 62."

Lesson 6

Field card

Signal types

  • Alarm: smoke, heat, pull station, waterflow
  • Supervisory: valve tamper, low air, fire pump running, most duct detectors
  • Trouble: open circuit, ground fault, AC loss, low battery, communicator failure

Panel keys

  • Acknowledge: "I've seen it." Quiets the panel buzzer.
  • Silence: stops the horns, but the panel stays in alarm
  • Reset: clears latched events. Restore the device first.

Before you test

  • Check in with the building contact
  • Document existing conditions on the panel
  • Know what the outputs control
  • Account on test, with the operator's number
  • Occupants notified

Before you leave

  • Every device restored and every bypass removed
  • Panel reset and normal
  • Signals confirmed with central, account off test
  • Deficiencies reviewed with the building contact
