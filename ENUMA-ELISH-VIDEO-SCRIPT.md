## 2. Full Narration (read straight through)

> Before earth or sky had names, before any god had risen, two waters existed: Apsu, the sweet water, and Tiamat, the restless salt sea, mingling their currents into one. From their union came the first stirrings of divinity — Lahmu and Lahamu, silt-born gods of the muddy deep, ancient and formless. Then Anshar and Kishar rose, the horizon of sky and the horizon of earth, vast circles binding the young cosmos together. From Anshar came Anu, lord of the high heavens, and from Anu came Ea, cunning and wise beyond all the gods who came before him.
>
> These younger gods gathered in noisy assembly, dancing and clamoring, and their tumult would not let old Apsu rest. Apsu, sleepless and wrathful, called his vizier Mummu to his side and vowed to destroy the children who troubled his ancient peace. Tiamat, mother of all, recoiled at the thought of destroying her own offspring, and begged Apsu to relent. But Apsu would not be swayed, and the gods above trembled, for the one who had given them life now sought their end.
>
> Ea, wisest of the gods, wove a spell of surpassing power, casting Apsu into an unbreakable sleep before he could strike. He seized the crown from Apsu's brow, and drew the waters of the deep as his own eternal dwelling. Upon the stilled body of Apsu, Ea built his shining house, and named it the Apsu, abode of wisdom beneath the world. There, with his consort Damkina, Ea fathered a son unlike any who had come before — a god of towering radiance. This child was Marduk, most glorious of gods: four eyes to see all things, four ears to hear all things, and fire upon his lips. He grew mighty beyond measure, and Anu, delighted, gave him the four winds to master and to hold.
>
> But the elder gods, grieving for Apsu and stirred by resentment, went to Tiamat and demanded vengeance. Tiamat's grief turned to fury, and she resolved to make war upon her own children, the gods of the younger heavens. She birthed an army of monsters — serpents with poison for blood, roaring dragons, scorpion-men, and raging storm-demons. Eleven terrors she made in all, and over them she set Kingu, her new consort, as supreme commander. Into Kingu's hands Tiamat placed the Tablet of Destinies, granting him command over the fate of all the gods. Word of this terrible army reached the young gods, and a great fear fell over the shining halls of heaven. Anshar sent forth Anu to face Tiamat, but even mighty Anu turned back, unable to stand before her rage. Then Ea himself went forward, but he too could not face the fury of the ancient sea, and returned in despair.
>
> At last Anshar looked to Marduk, youngest and mightiest, and asked if he alone would dare to face Tiamat. Marduk agreed, but demanded a price: that if he triumphed, he alone should be crowned king above all the gods. The gods gathered in council, feasting and drinking, and resolved to grant Marduk supreme and unshakable authority. To test his power, they set a single garment before him, and bid him destroy it and restore it with a word alone. He spoke, and the garment vanished; he spoke again, and it was whole — the gods rejoiced, and hailed him king. They gave him scepter, throne, and royal robe, and armed him with bow, arrow, mace, and net woven of lightning. The four winds became his chariot steeds, and he mounted a storm-chariot to ride against the mother of monsters.
>
> Marduk rode out alone across the boiling deep, the divine net gathered in his hand, the lightning bow at his side. Tiamat rose to meet him, vast and terrible, her army of monsters roaring in her wake, sky and sea trembling. She opened her jaws to devour him whole, certain that no god could stand before her ancient power. But Marduk drove the evil wind into her open mouth, and her jaws could not close upon him. Swiftly he cast his net, binding her within it, and loosed a single arrow straight into her heart. The arrow struck true, and the ancient mother of the deep gods fell, lifeless, into the churning sea. Her monstrous army scattered in terror, and Marduk bound them all, casting nets over the fleeing horde. Kingu, stripped of his stolen power, was seized and bound, the Tablet of Destinies torn from his grasp.
>
> Marduk returned to Tiamat's still body, vast as a mountain range, and split her in two like a great shell. Half of her he lifted high to become the vault of the sky, setting bolts and guards to hold back her waters. The other half he laid beneath, and it became the earth, firm ground for gods and creatures yet to come. From her eyes he loosed the Tigris and Euphrates, rivers flowing outward to nourish the new-made world. He raised her tail into the heavens as the Milky Way, and shaped mountains from the mounds of her breast. Marduk surveyed his new-formed world, sky above and earth below, and pronounced it good and well ordered.
>
> He set the great gods each within their stations, fixing the stars into constellations across the night. He appointed the twelve months of the year, one bright constellation to preside over each. He fashioned Nannar, the moon, to mark the night and measure the passage of days with his changing face. And he set the sun upon its course, dividing day from night in perfect, unbroken order. Thus the heavens themselves became a vast temple, engraved with the eternal decrees of the gods.
>
> The gods gathered once more, and Marduk asked which among the rebels had first incited the war. All pointed to Kingu, who had held the stolen Tablet and led Tiamat's monstrous host to war. Kingu was condemned, and the gods opened his veins, letting his blood fall dark upon the ground. From his blood, mixed with clay of the earth, Ea shaped the first of mankind, to labor in the gods' place. Mankind rose from the dust, freeing the gods forever from toil, tilling the earth and building temples. The gods rejoiced at their freedom, and turned their labor instead to building a home for their king.
>
> With their own hands the gods raised Babylon, city of gods, and at its heart built Esagila, Marduk's shining temple. There Marduk was enthroned above all, and the Anunnaki gods bowed low, swearing him eternal allegiance. The gods proclaimed his fifty names, each one a testament to his boundless and manifold power. He was named Lord of the Lands, Creator of Grain and Herbs, Bringer of Order to the trembling world. He was named the one who slew Tiamat, who shaped the heavens, who set the stars upon their paths. His name was to be remembered forever, sung by the wise, taught to the young, carried down through ages.
>
> So the Babylonians told of their world's beginning — not from nothing, but from struggle, sacrifice, and the ordering of chaos. Chaos was given form, the restless waters were tamed, and from conflict came the first fragile order of the world. This was the Enuma Elish — when on high the heavens had not yet been named, and the earth below had no name at all. A tale carved in clay, sung by an ancient people, of gods, monsters, and the birth of the ordered heavens.
